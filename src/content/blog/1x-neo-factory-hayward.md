**1X Technologies** has done the unglamorous thing humanoid startups keep promising: stand up a factory. On April 30, 2026 the company walked through its **NEO Factory** in **Hayward, California** — billed as **America’s first vertically integrated high-volume humanoid robot plant** — and said full-scale production is underway.

This is industry news with a fun punchline. Someone is actually building home humanoids in volume, not only posting balance videos.

## The Plant, in Numbers

From 1X’s own factory write-up:

- **58,000 sq ft** facility in Hayward
- **200+** employees on staff
- Stood up in about **three months** after final permits in January
- Initial capacity: up to **10,000 NEOs per year**
- Longer goal: **100,000+ units annually by end of 2027**, with a **San Carlos** site also coming online
- Vertical integration: motors, batteries, structures, transmissions, soft goods, sensors — built in-house where possible
- **17,000 motors** manufactured since production started (Revo2 tendon actuators)
- Battery line designed for **10,000 packs/year**, welding every **500 ms**
- Each NEO carries a **NEO Cortex** with **NVIDIA Jetson Thor**
- Current line output prioritizes **internal home testing**; customer deliveries still promised for **2026**

CEO **Bernt Børnich** framed the preorder shock: after an October 28 launch, 1X says it **booked the next year’s capacity — 10,000 NEOs — in five days**.

## What NEO Costs

On the [order page](https://www.1x.tech/order), pricing is public and simple:

- **$20,000** Early Access ownership (3-year warranty, premium support, priority delivery)
- **$499/month** subscription (starter productivity package, standard delivery)
- **$200** refundable deposit to start
- **U.S. deliveries start 2026**

Hardware claims on the same page include roughly **5'6"** height, **66 lb** weight, **4-hour** runtime on an **842 Wh** pack, walk/run speeds up to **1.4 / 6.2 m/s**, and a soft lattice body aimed at home safety.

## Robots Building Robots

1X says NEOs already help on the floor — stocking parts and basic logistics — while learning via the company’s world model. That is aspirational factory folklore until the ratios get public, but it is directionally interesting: the product is supposed to become part of its own supply chain.

## A Human's Take
