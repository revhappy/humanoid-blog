Boston Dynamics opened the Robotics Metaplant Application Center on September 21, 2026, inside Hyundai Motor Group Metaplant America outside Savannah, Georgia. Atlas robots are already in the building, training on real factory work rather than a stage routine.

The first job on the list is logistics. Boston Dynamics says the robots are learning to prepare and sequence automotive parts, then place them in the order the line needs. Component assembly is the later step. The company says that comes by 2030. After that, it wants Atlas on repetitive motion and heavy lifts.

The press photo of that cell shows a person in a headset on the near side of an orange barrier and an Atlas inside the cage, hands out, with parts racks behind it. Boston Dynamics captions the shot as a trainer teleoperating the robot. The company does not, in the announcement, spell out how much of the current work is teleop versus a policy running on its own.

Zack Jackowski, chief product and technology officer, said the center is part of a plan that covers supply chain, production, testing, and deployment.

> "Together with Hyundai Motor Group, we are pioneering physical AI at enterprise scale," Jackowski said. "RMAC reinforces our shared ambition to create a future where robots amplify human potential by making work safer and more productive."

The same release puts numbers on the rollout Hyundai has been talking about since CES. The group plans to start with 25,000 Atlas units at Hyundai Motor and Kia plants over the next few years, and to build a U.S. factory that can produce 30,000 robots a year. The Association for Advancing Automation's write-up of the opening repeats those figures and the sequencing task. Boston Dynamics' own "about" line on the release still describes Atlas as "currently in development."

Phase one is operating now. Next year the work moves into a new building at the same campus, about ten times the current size. Boston Dynamics says that larger hall is for automotive prep, and that in 2027 it also wants to try tasks with existing Spot and Stretch customers in manufacturing, aerospace, semiconductors, logistics, food and beverage, and life sciences. Those are discussions, not signed deployments.

## A Human's Take

A training hall next to a car plant is the right shape of problem. Sequencing racks is boring, measurable, and full of the edge cases a demo stage never has. I care less about the 25,000-unit sentence than about whether the robot in that cage can finish a parts order without a person in a headset holding the pose.

The release is honest in one quiet way: Atlas is still listed as in development, and the assembly work is dated 2030. Opening the building is the news. The unit target stays a plan until that sequencing cell runs without the trainer on the other side of the barrier.
