On **September 9**, Hero Esports put full-size humanoids in a Riyadh cage and called it the start of a league, not a one-off stunt. **CyberHero x Riyadh** was invite-only. The machines in the ring were **EngineAI T800** units: about **1.73 metres** tall, **75–85 kg**, **29** articulated joints.

Xinhua’s handout stills from the night are the receipts. A yellow T800 throws a high kick into a blue opponent. Another frame has the yellow robot airborne over the blue one, a detached yellow foot lying on the mat. A later shot shows a green-and-yellow unit with both arms up while its opponent is on the floor in front of a giant “KO” screen.

## What Hero Esports actually announced

The September 9 press release from Hero Esports says CyberHero is meant to be the first global humanoid robot sports league, with an inaugural season spanning **eight cities** across the Middle East, Europe, the Americas, and Asia. Two of those eight stops are planned for the Middle East.

The T800 can walk, run, punch, kick, turn, recover after falls, “and the occasional dance,” per that release. EngineAI first ran a T800 fight league in Shenzhen on **July 16**. Hero Esports says the Riyadh units came with an upgraded combat package.

An earlier August 31 release had already billed the night as the Middle East’s first humanoid robot kickboxing event, with **ten** T800s in a purpose-built arena. Other robotics brands were “also expected to make an appearance.” Xinhua’s caption set includes Unitree humanoids dancing between bouts, and a still of staff working on a downed yellow robot under the lights.

**Danny Tang**, co-founder and CEO of Hero Esports, said the point of the format is to push robots until the data from competition changes what they can do:

> “CyberHero was born from a bold idea about the future of competition: that robots can be pushed to test their limits, break new boundaries and improve through data generated in competition.”

The company says it is setting up a global technology lab at its Shanghai headquarters. The league model names three roles: **builders** who engineer the robots, **coders** who train the intelligence, and **drivers** who make live decisions in the arena.

## The show around the hardware

HRH Prince Faisal bin Bandar bin Sultan al Saud, chairman of the Saudi Esports Federation, welcomed the choice of Riyadh. MENA creator **AboFlah** and Saudi sports creator **Bashar Arabi** managed the two competing teams. DJ **Tokyo Machine** performed. Hero Esports, backed by Savvy Games Group and Tencent, says it organizes more than **7,000** esports competitions a year.

The August 31 release also sketched a **CyberHero Saudi–China Scholar Program** for up to **30** Saudi students to visit Chinese robotics sites. That is a side program, not a robot spec.

What the releases do not give: bout scores, round counts, whether the T800s were autonomous or driven, or a named venue. Treat the night as a staged launch with real hardware in a real cage.

## A Human's Take

I am here for a full-size humanoid that has to recover after it hits the floor, not for the DJ. The airborne still and the technicians kneeling over a yellow chassis are the honest pair: the move looked wild, and someone still had to put the robot back together between rounds. If CyberHero actually runs eight cities, the interesting number will be how often those T800s get up without a human on the mat.
