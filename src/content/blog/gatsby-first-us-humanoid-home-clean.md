Another San Francisco cleaning story, different pitch. **Gatsby** (under **West Egg Labs**) says a humanoid completed what it calls the **first consumer home cleaning by a humanoid robot in the United States**. Fox News CyberGuy coverage describes a waitlist customer booked through Gatsby’s **iOS app**, with the robot working the full apartment.

## The Offer

According to that report (company claims via Gatsby):

- **Flat $150 per clean**, any apartment size — no tips or hidden fees, per Gatsby
- Typical clean about **3 hours** (one run cited: about 8:42 a.m. to 11:47 a.m.)
- Tasks described: dishes, surfaces, floors, bed-making, laundry folding — more than a robot vacuum
- **No human cleaner physically in the home** during the visit
- Harder tasks may still use **remote teleoperation**; routine work is described as autonomous
- Service **San Francisco only** for now, waitlist for other cities

Gatsby positions itself as a **service and distribution layer** — robot-agnostic, so it can swap hardware as better bodies show up — rather than only selling one brand of humanoid.

Founder and CEO **Aron Frishberg**, quoted in the Fox piece, frames housework as unpaid time people would rather spend elsewhere — the product pitch is time back, not just shiny hardware.

## The Catch (the interesting part)

Homes are chaos: chairs, cords, pets, laundry piles. Gatsby says customers are covered if the robot breaks something — still read the fine print. Privacy is the other door: remote operators, mapping data, what gets recorded. The CyberGuy write-up flags those questions without claiming Gatsby is doing anything wrong; they are the right questions for any connected robot in a private space.

## A Human's Take

Tau sells an hour at $30 with an invite code. Gatsby sells a whole-apartment visit at $150. Different products, same city, same idea: **book a robot service**, do not buy a $50k roommate. Both still lean on human operators in some form. That is not a fail — it is how early services stay useful. The fun part is that SF is suddenly a live lab for home humanoids; the serious part is privacy and reliability when the novelty wears off.
