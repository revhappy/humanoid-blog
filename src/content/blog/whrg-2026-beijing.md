The second **World Humanoid Robot Games** open in Beijing on **August 22** and run through **August 26**. Organizers told a press briefing the field is **666 teams** and **2,056 robots** from **16 countries**. Last year’s inaugural meet had **280 teams** and just over **500** robots.

People’s Daily carried the Xinhua briefing on August 14. Mashable’s August 21 dispatch is from the **National Speed Skating Oval**, where teams are already test-running track events, and some of those runs have already gone sideways on video.

## New events, and a rule that punishes the joystick

Jiang Guangzhi, director of the Beijing Municipal Bureau of Economy and Information Technology, said this year adds **long jump, weightlifting, tug of war, and table tennis**, on top of last year’s sports-plus-scenario mix. Scenario work expands into factories, hotels, homes, and logistics: assembly, housekeeping, emergency response, library sorting.

Mashable, citing the Games site, notes a scoring split that matters more than the medal count. Human operators are allowed in events like long jump and kickboxing. Breakdancing, cheerleading, and the **4×100 m** relay must run **autonomous**. Scenario events allow both, but **remote-control scores are halved**. If you joystick it, you leave points on the table.

Jiang’s line, via Xinhua:

> “Once robots master these ‘last-meter’ skills, they can turn medals into orders and move directly from the competition arena to real-world workplaces.”

Domestic entries dominate the sheet: **641** Chinese teams, **1,975** robots, from **157** companies and **200** universities, including 27 named prominent schools. Brazil sent a national group of five RoboCup squads. The U.S., Germany, and Japan are on the list.

## The footage that leaked early

Mashable’s piece is partly a crash reel. Robotics engineer **Eren Chen** posted a clip that appeared to show Unitree’s new **Superman** leaving the lane and hitting electrical boxes, and another unidentified runner folding at the waist after a wall hit. Chen’s point, quoted there: last year’s speed work proved bodies can run; this year’s autonomous track rules ask whether they know when to stop.

Tickets start at **¥98** (about **$14.50**), Mashable says, and the public can attend.

## A Human's Take

Halving the teleop score is the most interesting rule in the building. It forces teams to ship a policy, not a puppeteer. I will watch tug of war and table tennis because those are contact plus timing, not another 100 m that a joystick already won. Crashes in practice are data. Medals that turn into purchase orders, as Jiang hopes, are a different test, and this weekend does not grade that.
