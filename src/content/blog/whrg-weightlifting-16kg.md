Weightlifting made its debut at the second World Humanoid Robot Games in Beijing, and the inaugural title went to a **TianGong** from the joint **Beijing Innovation Center of Humanoid Robotics** and **Huazhong University of Science and Technology** team. The robot started at **15 kg** and finished a **16 kg** overhead lift.

TechNode, citing a competition report, names the same Beijing Humanoid–HUST squad. Global Times’ English account posted that the lift was on August 24 and called the robot a TianGong. Bastille’s wire, from China Global Television Network’s feed, is the piece with the rules and the quotes.

## The lift, by the book

Bastille’s rules are specific:

- Grasp the barbell with **both hands**
- Lift on a **strictly vertical** path
- Hold it overhead for at least **two seconds**
- Only the **feet** may touch the platform; torso, arms, or knees on the deck fail the attempt
- Rankings use the **heaviest successful single lift**

Every robot has to be a full humanoid, not a thick-based lifter. **Wen Han**, on the Beijing center–HUST team, told the wire why they care about that shape:

> "The reason why we focus on humanoid structures is that many robots with [thicker] bases [instead of legs] can't lift such heavy weights, while humanoid robots can utilize the full-body torque to lift, giving them a higher potential limit."

Teams used dexterous hands with at least three fingers. Some wrapped the palms in tape. Wen said they picked agile hands and reinforced them for load.

## The other clip from the same floor

The 16 kg gold is not the clip most people saw first. TechTimes and NDTV report a different robot that lost balance on a **15 kg** attempt and lurched toward the judges’ table. Officials stopped that try. Four robots entered the heavyweight class, each starting at 15 kg, according to that same TechTimes write-up. TechTimes also says weightlifting still **permits teleoperation**, unlike the flat-track sprints.

Sixteen kilograms is not a human clean-and-jerk. It is a first gold in a new event built to stress grip, joint torque, and whether a biped can keep the bar over its head without sitting down.
