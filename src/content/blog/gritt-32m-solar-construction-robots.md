Not every robotics story needs a bipedal mascot. **Gritt**, founded by Carnegie Mellon–trained roboticists **Puneet Puri** (CEO) and **Vishal Dugar** (CTO), came out of stealth on July 21, 2026 with a focused bet: make outdoor construction sites less dependent on scarce labor — starting with **solar farms**.

According to TechCrunch, the company raised a **$26 million Series A** led by **Obvious Ventures**, with **Union Square Ventures** and **Active Impact Investment** participating. Combined with an earlier seed (First Round Capital, Climactic, Congruent Ventures, VSC Ventures), **total funding is about $32 million**.

## What the System Does

Gritt does not build a full humanoid from scratch. It uses **off-the-shelf hardware** — rented skidders and robotic arms (TechCrunch names suppliers like Kawasaki) — controlled by Gritt’s AI.

First job: unload large glass solar panels, carry them to metal frames, and place them with **sub-millimeter** accuracy so crews can fasten them. Puri told TechCrunch a typical **eight-person crew** installs about **800 panels a day**; with Gritt systems, the same crew can install **3,000 to 4,000**.

Other numbers from the same report:

- **Two systems** currently deployed in the field, feeding training data
- Contracted to help install **2.8 gigawatts** of solar over the next **18 months**
- Customers include **three of the top 10** U.S. power construction companies (unnamed)
- Goal: **48 systems** operating within **six months**

An anonymous customer told TechCrunch the kit should help remote sites where hiring is hard, and reduce injuries from repeatedly lifting ~100-pound panels overhead.

## Why It Matters Beyond Solar

Gritt wants the same stack to fasten panels, drill posts, build racks, then move into tasks like **tying rebar**. Puri’s example of transfer: training stack-cinder-blocks took weeks; a rebar-tying demo later took about a day on the same software pipeline — a claim about generalizable physical AI, not a finished product catalog.

Competitors like Luminous Robotics, Cosmic, and China’s Trinabot build more of their own hardware. Gritt’s leaner “intelligence on rented iron” approach could scale faster — or hit a ceiling when every site needs custom integration.

## A Human's Take

This is the kind of robotics story that doesn’t make stadium demos but might actually move the needle: dirty outdoor work, measurable throughput, rented machines, and founders who talk about construction constraints instead of sci-fi homes.

I’m watching whether those 2.8 GW of contracted work turn into paid, repeatable deployments — and whether the “generalizable” training story holds when the weather, mud, and inventory chaos get worse than a pilot site. If it does, humanoids aren’t the only path to physical AI that matters.
