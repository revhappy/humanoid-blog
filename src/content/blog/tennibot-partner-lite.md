**Tennibot**, an Auburn, Alabama robotics shop, opened orders for **Partner Lite**, a stationary AI ball machine priced at **$1,495** for the first weeks. The company’s Lite pages put intro pricing through **24 September 2026**. **Partner V2**, the version that drives around the court, stays at **$2,275**. The $780 cut is the drive base. Software, app, and custom drills are the same stack.

Orders are open now. Tennibot’s FAQ and product pages say each unit is built to order in Auburn, with a **60-day** trial, **three-year** warranty, free US shipping and returns, and no subscription for the app, drills, or over-the-air updates.

## Same brain, stays put

Tennibot’s one-line: “Same brain, no legs.”

From the Lite spec and product pages:

- **About 25 lb** (the company says half of a typical 45–55 lb machine)
- **15 × 10 × 19 in**
- **10–70 mph** ball speed (65 mph cap on the pickleball SKU)
- **3,600 rpm** max spin
- **140-ball** hopper
- **4–5 hour** battery, **10.5 Ah** pack
- **8 TOPS** on-device inference, 4K cameras, 3D ball tracking
- Shot-type transitions under **1.5 seconds**, via upgraded braking motors
- Apple Watch control through a paired iPhone
- Tennis and padel share one unit; pickleball is a dedicated machine

It works with **Rover**, Tennibot’s autonomous ball collector. The Lite + Rover bundle is **$3,245**, **$525** off buying them separate. Rover is **$2,275** on its own.

The Lite pages are clear about the missing feature: V2 repositions itself so feeds come from where a hitting partner would stand. Lite sits where you put it. For stroke grooves and lessons, Tennibot says that is enough.

## A US product with a date on the price

Tennibot’s about page: the company designs and assembles the machines in the United States. The FAQ says tennis and padel Lite are the same hardware; pick pickleball if that is your sport. Shipping for new orders is on the order of **six to seven weeks** in the FAQ; intro coverage also pointed to late September as the start of shipments.

What the company does **not** publish on those pages: a locked post-intro price, independent accuracy numbers versus cheaper pattern machines, or Lite reliability stats. The claim for “AI custom drills” is that the app builds sessions; there is no public study comparing that to a programmed drill book.

## A Human's Take

A $1,495 machine you can carry in one hand is a better commercial story than another $20,000 court robot that needs a van. Stripping the drive base and keeping the cameras is an honest SKU. I still want to know whether the “AI drills” beat a $400 dumb feeder on a stopwatch, not on a landing page. If they do, clubs will buy two Lites and one Rover and stop arguing about it.
