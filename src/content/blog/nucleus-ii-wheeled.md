**Nucleus** came out of stealth in August on **Unitree G1** bipeds. A month later, co-founder **Melvin Schwarz** posted that the next units on a real logistics floor have no legs.

**Nucleus II** is a dual-arm upper body on a wheeled mobile base. Schwarz’s September 8 update lists the customer ask in three lines: more payload (**10 kg+**), more uptime (**5–9 h**), and more safety — “No legs!!!” Humanoids Daily reported the same numbers the next day: **up to 10 kg per arm**, **six times** the battery capacity of Nucleus I, and **five to nine hours** of continuous operation.

## What the factory actually asked for

Schwarz wrote that demand moved faster than the team expected, and that plant managers were blunt:

> “Only the factory tells you what to build!!! You can spend weeks making something work perfectly in the office/lab, then discover a completely different set of problems within the first few hours of a real deployment.”

Humanoids Daily frames the wheeled chassis as an answer to three biped limits: tote weight, shift length, and tipping. The company has not named the wheeled-base manufacturer. HD says Nucleus is adding custom 3D-printed parts and brackets to a third-party chassis. Schwarz says Nucleus is not just buying hardware — it is pushing requirements back to a partner and contributing to the next versions.

The software story is the same stack on new metal. Nucleus bills **robots-as-a-service** for completed physical tasks. When the robot hits an edge case, remote operators take over. HD notes recent teleop additions: haptic vibration on contact, force feedback scaled by arm-positioning error, and visual force indicators. Interventions go into the training pipeline. The company says it wants **up to 25 robots** deployed by the end of Q1.

The company site still sells the original pitch: scan a factory, simulate it, then run supervised labor by the hour. It does not yet list Nucleus II as a named product. The hardware-agnostic line is on the site: Nucleus “select[s] across manufacturers and modify the hardware heavily, based on your task on site.”

What is missing: a named customer, a cycle time, and the base OEM. Schwarz’s next promised clip is a continuous multi-hour shift, including the failures.

## A Human's Take

I care less about the branding change than the customer veto. A plant that asks for “no legs” is telling you the biped is still a reliability and safety problem on flat concrete. A 5–9 hour shift at 10 kg per arm, without a human catching a fall, would be a more useful datapoint than another G1 in a branded suit. Schwarz promised that clip next, failures included. That is the one I want.
