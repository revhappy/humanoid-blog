The second **World Humanoid Robot Games** just posted a roster that is hard to ignore. Organizers told a Beijing media conference on **August 13** that **666 teams** and **2,056 robots** from **16 countries** will compete **August 22–26**. That is about four times last year’s turnout.

**Jiang Guangzhi**, director of the Beijing Municipal Bureau of Economy and Information Technology, said the field includes teams from the United States, Germany, and Japan. Brazil is sending a national team built from **five RoboCup squads**.

## What they will actually do

The program is more than a 100-meter dash. Organizers say this year has **more than 30 events**. New ones include **long jump**, **weightlifting**, **tug of war**, and **table tennis**, which Jiang said are there to stress motion control, structure, and core parts.

The other half of the schedule is supposed to look like work. Scenario contests expand into mock **factories, hotels, homes, and logistics** sites. Tasks listed by Xinhua include assembling, housekeeping, emergency response, and sorting books in a library setup.

Domestic numbers dominate the grid. Beijing Review, citing Jiang, put Chinese participation at **641 teams** and **1,975 robots** from **157 enterprises** and **200 universities and research institutions**, including **27** major Chinese universities.

> “Once robots master these ‘last-meter’ skills, they can turn medals into orders and move directly from the competition arena to real-world workplaces,” Jiang said, according to Xinhua via Beijing Review.

The first games, in **August 2025**, drew **280 teams** and more than **500** humanoids from 16 countries.

## Why the scale jumped

ANews, citing Xinhua, notes a Chinese official said in **July** that the country’s humanoid output is expected to exceed **100,000 units** this year. The same report cites **Smart Analytics Global** as saying Chinese makers accounted for more than **97 percent** of global humanoid shipments in the **first half of 2026**.

That is the commercial backdrop, not the scoreboard. Last year’s Guardian write-up of the inaugural meet is a useful reminder of what the spectacle still looks like: kickboxers falling over missed roundhouses, a 1,500-meter runner whose **head came off**, and plenty of handlers hauling robots off the floor to recharge.

## A Human's Take

I want the new events more than the headcount. Tug-of-war and hotel housekeeping are uglier problems than a staged 100-meter. If a robot can keep its feet in a pull and still fold towels without punching the linen cart, that is a better receipt than another formation photo. I’ll be watching how many of those “last-meter” tasks finish without a human dragging the machine offstage.
